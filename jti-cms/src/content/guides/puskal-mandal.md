interview:
- q: How did you come to specialise in Manas specifically?
  a: I grew up in a village on the park boundary, so Manas was always just there, in the background of everything. When the park began actively rebuilding after some very difficult years, I wanted to be part of that story rather than just watch it happen. I've guided almost nowhere else since.
- q: A place you keep returning to?
  a: The grasslands near Kokilabari, close to the Bhutan foothills. It's where I've had my best sightings of the black panther — and honestly just where I like to sit with a cup of tea before a drive and listen to the park wake up.
- q: A moment with a traveller that's stayed with you?
  a: We once had the panther cross the track ahead of us in the open, for perhaps three seconds, and the vehicle went completely silent — not a single camera clicked. Afterwards a guest told me she'd forgotten to take a single photo because she didn't want to look away, even for a second. I understood exactly what she meant.
- q: What do most visitors not expect about Manas?
  a: How much has come back. A lot of people still think of it as the park that struggled, not the park that recovered. Tigers, rhinos, wild buffalo, even this rare cat — it's all here again, and I like being the one who gets to tell that story on a jeep at six in the morning.
- q: What do you hope people take home?
  a: Respect for how fragile and how resilient this place has proven to be, both at once. A good sighting is memorable. Understanding what it took to make that sighting possible again — that's what I actually want people to leave with.
