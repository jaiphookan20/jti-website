interview:
- q: How did you start guiding culture tours?
  a: I trained as a textile designer and spent years working with weaving cooperatives around Sualkuchi. Visitors kept asking, informally, if I could show them around, and eventually that became the job. I still think of myself as a textile person first and a guide second.
- q: A place you keep returning to?
  a: A small Mishing weaving village not far from Majuli, where three generations of one family still work on the same loom pit. I've brought dozens of groups there over the years, and the family always finds something new to show me too — that exchange is what keeps it interesting.
- q: A moment with a traveller that's stayed with you?
  a: A guest once asked a weaver how long a particular gamosa had taken to make. When she said eleven days, he went very quiet, then asked if he could sit with her for the rest of the afternoon and just watch. He missed the next stop on our itinerary entirely — and it was absolutely the right call.
- q: What surprises visitors most about this region's culture?
  a: How alive it all still is. People sometimes expect "cultural tourism" to mean museums and performances staged for visitors. Here, you're mostly just standing in someone's actual workday — a tea pluck, a loom, a kitchen — and that honesty is what people respond to.
- q: What do you hope people take home?
  a: A sense of the Northeast as a place people actually live, not just a landscape to look at. If someone leaves knowing the name of a weaver or a tea-picker they met, rather than just a place they visited, I've done my job.
