interview:
- q: How did you become a naturalist?
  a: I studied zoology, half by accident, and ended up doing fieldwork on hog deer in Kaziranga for my dissertation. I never really left. Guiding felt like a natural next step — I already had the knowledge, I just needed to learn how to share it without turning every drive into a lecture.
- q: A place you keep returning to?
  a: The Western Range in Kaziranga, early morning, right after the mist starts to lift. Rhinos are creatures of habit, and if you know their patterns you can predict, almost to the minute, when one will step out of the grass. It never stops being a thrill, even after hundreds of mornings.
- q: A moment with a traveller that's stayed with you?
  a: I once guided a birder who'd waited thirty years to see a Bengal florican. We found one on our second morning, and she didn't say a word for almost a minute — just watched through her binoculars with her hand over her mouth. Moments like that are the whole reason I do this.
- q: What surprises visitors most?
  a: How much is happening in the grass itself, not just the big animals. People come for rhinos and tigers and leave talking about the birdlife, the insects, the sheer density of life in that floodplain. Kaziranga rewards people who look closely.
- q: What do you hope people take home?
  a: An appreciation for how hard-won this landscape is. Kaziranga's rhino population was almost wiped out in the last century. Every sighting is really a small victory, and I want people to leave understanding that.
