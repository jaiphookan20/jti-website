interview:
- q: How did you learn to identify birds by call?
  a: Mostly by getting it wrong, for years, in front of people who knew better. I started as a porter on birding expeditions in my early twenties, and the visiting ornithologists were generous with their knowledge. I'd write calls down phonetically in a notebook at night and test myself the next morning.
- q: A place you keep returning to?
  a: The Eaglenest ridge road, especially the stretch above Lama Camp. The elevation change over such a short distance means you can move through three or four different bird communities in a single morning. I don't think there's anywhere quite like it in India.
- q: A moment with a traveller that's stayed with you?
  a: A group from Massachusetts once spent four days with me chasing a Bugun liocichla — a bird only described to science in 2006, found nowhere else on Earth. When we finally found it, one of them, a retired professor, simply shook my hand and said "thank you" about six times. I still remember exactly where we were standing.
- q: What do most visitors not expect about Arunachal?
  a: How much altitude changes everything in a single day — breakfast in subtropical forest, dinner in temperate cloud forest, a completely different set of birds at each. People arrive expecting one Northeast and discover it's really a dozen, stacked on top of each other.
- q: What do you hope people take home?
  a: Patience with silence. The best birding mornings are quiet ones — long stretches where nothing happens, and then everything happens at once. I want people to learn to enjoy the waiting, not just the reward.
