interview:
- q: How did you end up guiding in the mountains?
  a: I grew up in the valley, so the mountains were never a destination for me — they were just where I lived. It took travellers coming from outside to show me how extraordinary that was. I started driving for a cousin's outfit in my twenties, and somewhere on one of those early trips I realised I didn't want to do anything else.
- q: Is there a place in Ladakh you keep returning to?
  a: The road along Tso Moriri, past Chumathang. It's a long, quiet stretch, and the light changes so completely by the hour that I've driven it a hundred times and never seen it the same way twice. I always try to time it so we arrive as the lake turns that particular blue.
- q: A moment with a traveller that's stayed with you?
  a: A few years ago I had a couple who'd been trying to start a family for a long time, and somewhere near Hemis they just stopped the car and cried — not from sadness, they said, just from how small and quiet everything suddenly felt. I still think about that. The mountains do that to people.
- q: What do most visitors get wrong about Ladakh before they arrive?
  a: They expect it to feel remote and empty. It's the opposite — every pass has a story, every monastery is still lived in, not a museum. I spend a lot of my first day just trying to convince people to slow down and look properly.
- q: What do you hope people take home with them?
  a: Patience, mostly. Ladakh doesn't perform for you on a schedule. The people who leave happiest are the ones who let the place set the pace, instead of the other way round.
